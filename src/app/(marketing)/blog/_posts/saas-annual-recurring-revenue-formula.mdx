ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) is the most cited metric in SaaS — but it's also one of the most frequently miscalculated. Investors ask for it in every pitch. Board decks center on it. And yet, many founders report a number that doesn't hold up under scrutiny.

Getting ARR wrong doesn't just mean bad data. It means bad decisions — hiring too fast, spending too aggressively, or misjudging your runway. This guide covers the exact formula, worked examples, the components that make up ARR, and the mistakes that skew your numbers.

## The ARR Formula

At its core, the formula is straightforward:

```
ARR = MRR × 12
```

**MRR** (Monthly Recurring Revenue) is your total recurring revenue from active subscriptions, normalized to a monthly figure. Multiply by 12 to get ARR.

**Example:**
- January MRR: $12,500
- ARR: $12,500 × 12 = $150,000

But the real calculation requires more nuance than a single multiplication. You need to account for different subscription types, mid-month changes, and what actually qualifies as "recurring."

### Per-Customer ARR Calculation

The most accurate way to calculate ARR is to compute it per customer, then sum:

```
Customer A (Monthly): $99/month → $1,188 ARR
Customer B (Monthly): $299/month → $3,588 ARR
Customer C (Annual): $2,400/year → $2,400 ARR
Customer D (Monthly): $49/month → $588 ARR

Total ARR: $1,188 + $3,588 + $2,400 + $588 = $7,764
```

Note that Customer C's annual plan is already expressed as a yearly amount — no multiplication needed.

### ARR Components: Breaking Down the Growth

ARR isn't just a snapshot number. The best way to understand what's driving your ARR is to decompose it into four components:

| Component | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **New ARR** | ARR from new customers acquired this period | 5 new customers at $99/mo = $5,940 new ARR |
| **Expansion ARR** | ARR gained from existing customers upgrading | 3 customers upgrade from $49 to $99/mo = $1,764 expansion ARR |
| **Contraction ARR** | ARR lost from existing customers downgrading | 2 customers downgrade from $299 to $99/mo = $4,800 contraction ARR |
| **Churned ARR** | ARR lost from customers cancelling entirely | 1 customer at $199/mo cancels = $2,388 churned ARR |

**Net New ARR = New ARR + Expansion ARR − Contraction ARR − Churned ARR**

Using the examples above:

```
Net New ARR = $5,940 + $1,764 − $4,800 − $2,388 = $516
```

A positive Net New ARR means your recurring revenue is growing. A negative number means you're shrinking — even if total revenue looks fine on paper (because one-time income might mask the decline).

## Why ARR Does Not Equal Annual Revenue

This is one of the most common points of confusion, and it trips up even experienced founders.

| Metric | What It Measures | Time Orientation |
|---|---|---|
| ARR | Current run rate, annualized | Forward-looking |
| Annual Revenue | Cash actually received in a year | Backward-looking |
| Bookings | Value of new contracts signed | Point-in-time |

**ARR is a projection.** It tells you: "If every active subscriber pays as expected for the next 12 months, this is what we'll earn." It's not a guarantee. It's a run rate.

**Annual Revenue is historical.** It's the actual cash that hit your bank account over the past 12 months. It includes one-time fees, refunds, and non-recurring income.

**Bookings are commitments.** A customer signs a $24,000 annual contract. That's a booking. But if they pay monthly, only $2,000 of that is ARR.

Confusing these metrics leads to inflated projections, mispriced equity, and investor conversations that go sideways fast.

## ARR for Different Subscription Types

### Monthly Subscriptions

The simplest case. Multiply the monthly rate by 12:

```
Customer A: $99/month = $1,188 ARR
Customer B: $299/month = $3,588 ARR
```

### Annual Subscriptions

Do NOT multiply by 12 — you'd be double-counting. If a customer pays $2,400/year upfront, their ARR contribution is $2,400. The annual payment is already expressed in annual terms.

```
Customer C pays $2,400/year upfront = $2,400 ARR (already annual)
```

### Usage-Based or Hybrid Models

Some SaaS products charge a base subscription plus usage overages. For ARR purposes:

- **Base subscription:** Include the full amount
- **Variable usage:** Include only if there's a contractual minimum. Pure pay-as-you-go revenue without a floor is not recurring — don't count it in ARR

Example: A customer pays $200/month base plus variable usage averaging $50/month. If there's no contractual minimum on the usage portion, ARR = $2,400 (base only). If there's a $30/month minimum on usage, ARR = $2,400 + $360 = $2,760.

### Multi-Year Contracts

For multi-year deals, ARR reflects the annualized rate — not the total contract value (TCV).

```
3-year contract, $60,000 total → $20,000/year → $20,000 ARR
```

TCV is useful for cash flow planning, but ARR should always reflect the annual run rate.

## The 5 Most Common ARR Mistakes

### 1. Including One-Time Revenue

Setup fees, onboarding charges, consulting revenue, and professional services are not recurring. If a customer pays $5,000 for implementation and $99/month for the product, their ARR is $1,188 — not $7,188.

**Fix:** Create a strict rule: if it happens once, it doesn't count. Only include revenue that repeats on a predictable cadence.

### 2. Using Gross vs Net Revenue

Gross ARR includes all subscription revenue before refunds, chargebacks, and payment processing fees. Net ARR subtracts those. Most investors want net ARR. Your Stripe dashboard shows gross by default.

**Fix:** Always clarify which you're reporting. If you don't specify, readers will assume gross — which means your "real" ARR is lower than what you stated.

### 3. Not Normalizing Annual Plans

If a customer pays $1,200/year in January and cancels in March, you can't count the full $1,200 as ARR for the year. Their actual recurring contribution was only $300 (3 months × $100/month).

**Fix:** When computing ARR at a point in time, only include revenue from customers with active subscriptions. If a customer cancels mid-period, their ARR contribution drops to zero immediately.

### 4. Including Trials and Freemium Users

ARR is recurring revenue only. Free trials, freemium tiers, and beta users don't generate revenue — until they convert.

**Fix:** Only count ARR from paying, active subscribers. If you want to track the revenue potential of your free users, use a separate metric like "potential ARR" or "pipeline ARR."

### 5. Snapshotting ARR on the Wrong Date

ARR is a point-in-time metric. If you calculate it on the 1st of the month vs the 15th, you might get different numbers because of mid-month churn or new signups.

**Fix:** Measure ARR at the same time each month — ideally at month-end or month-start. Consistency matters more than the specific date. Pick one and stick with it.

## ARR vs MRR: When to Use Each

Both metrics are valuable, but they serve different purposes:

**Use MRR for operational decisions:**
- Month-to-month growth analysis
- Churn rate calculations
- Pricing experiments
- Short-term cash flow planning

**Use ARR for strategic decisions:**
- Investor reporting and fundraising
- Company valuation
- Year-over-year benchmarking
- Board reporting

Most SaaS companies report both. MRR gives you granularity; ARR gives you the big picture.

## ARR-Based Valuation Benchmarks

ARR is the primary metric investors use to value SaaS companies. Multiples vary based on growth rate, retention, and market conditions:

| Stage | ARR Multiple | Typical Profile |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-traction / bootstrapped | 2–4x ARR | under $50K ARR, founder-led |
| Early traction | 5–8x ARR | $50K–$500K ARR, 10–20% MoM growth |
| Growth stage | 8–15x ARR | $500K–$5M ARR, strong retention |
| High-growth, VC-backed | 15–30x ARR | $5M+ ARR, 20%+ MoM growth, under 2% churn |

These are rough ranges. Actual multiples depend on your NRR (net revenue retention), CAC payback, and market conditions. A company with 120%+ NRR can command a premium even at lower revenue levels.

## Real-World Example: ARR for a $1M ARR SaaS

Let's say you run a B2B SaaS with the following subscriber base:

| Customer | Plan | Billing | Monthly | Annual |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 customers | Starter | Monthly | $49 | — |
| 30 customers | Growth | Monthly | $149 | — |
| 10 customers | Growth | Annual | — | $1,490 |
| 5 customers | Enterprise | Monthly | $499 | — |
| 2 customers | Enterprise | Annual | — | $4,990 |

**Monthly ARR contributions:**
- Starter: 50 × $49 × 12 = $29,400
- Growth (monthly): 30 × $149 × 12 = $53,640
- Growth (annual): 10 × $1,490 = $14,900
- Enterprise (monthly): 5 × $499 × 12 = $29,940
- Enterprise (annual): 2 × $4,990 = $9,980

**Total ARR: $137,860**

If you only looked at monthly revenue, you'd see $11,488/month. But ARR gives you the annualized view that investors and benchmarks use.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between ARR and MRR?

MRR is your monthly recurring revenue. ARR is MRR × 12 — an annualized projection. MRR is better for month-to-month operational analysis (churn, growth rate, pricing experiments). ARR is better for investor reporting, valuation, and long-term benchmarking.

### Should I include add-ons and overages in my ARR?

Include add-ons only if they're contractual and recurring (e.g., a monthly seat add-on). One-time overages or variable usage without a contractual floor should not be included in ARR.

### How do I calculate ARR if I have both monthly and annual subscribers?

Calculate ARR per customer: monthly subscribers contribute (monthly rate × 12), annual subscribers contribute their annual rate directly. Sum all customer contributions to get total ARR. Don't multiply annual plans by 12 — that double-counts.

### When should I report ARR to investors?

Report ARR at every investor touchpoint — pitch decks, board updates, monthly updates. Investors expect it. Make sure you report it consistently (same measurement date each period) and clarify whether it's gross or net ARR.

### What's a good ARR growth rate for early-stage SaaS?

For pre-seed and seed-stage SaaS, aim for 15–25% MoM ARR growth. That translates to roughly 4–14x ARR growth year-over-year. At later stages, 5–10% MoM is considered strong.
